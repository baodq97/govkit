# The Business Model Canvas

Nine blocks describing how an organisation creates, delivers and captures value. Designed by
Business Model Foundry AG (Osterwalder & Pigneur), licensed CC BY-SA 3.0 —
<https://www.strategyzer.com/canvas/business-model-canvas>.

The prompting questions below are the canvas's own. Use them verbatim in an interview; they have
been sharpened over two decades and are better than anything improvised in the moment.

## Fill order

The canvas is drawn left-to-right but **filled right-to-left**: start with Customer Segments and
Value Propositions, because every block to the left of them is a consequence. A canvas assembled
from Key Partners forward tends to describe the org chart rather than the business.

For the DDD purpose, three blocks carry the most weight downstream — Value Propositions, Customer
Segments, Revenue Streams. Spend the session's energy there.

## 1. Customer Segments

> For whom are we creating value? Who are our most important customers?

Types: mass market · niche market · segmented · diversified · multi-sided platform.

**Boundary signal:** two segments whose needs genuinely differ often want two models, not one
model with a type flag. When someone says "it's the same thing, just for enterprise", ask what
changes — the answer is usually a different language, which is a different context.

## 2. Value Propositions

> What value do we deliver to the customer? Which one of our customer's problems are we helping to
> solve? What bundles of products and services are we offering to each Customer Segment? Which
> customer needs are we satisfying?

Characteristics: newness · performance · customization · "getting the job done" · design ·
brand/status · price · cost reduction · risk reduction · accessibility · convenience/usability.

**Boundary signal:** the capabilities that *deliver* the value proposition are the core-domain
candidates. This block is the single strongest input to core/supporting/generic classification.

## 3. Channels

> Through which Channels do our Customer Segments want to be reached? How are we reaching them
> now? How are our Channels integrated? Which ones work best? Which ones are most cost-efficient?
> How are we integrating them with customer routines?

Channel phases: 1 awareness · 2 evaluation · 3 purchase · 4 delivery · 5 after sales.

## 4. Customer Relationships

> What type of relationship does each of our Customer Segments expect us to establish and maintain
> with them? Which ones have we established? How are they integrated with the rest of our business
> model? How costly are they?

Examples: personal assistance · dedicated personal assistance · self-service · automated services ·
communities · co-creation.

## 5. Revenue Streams

> For what value are our customers really willing to pay? For what do they currently pay? How are
> they currently paying? How would they prefer to pay? How much does each Revenue Stream contribute
> to overall revenues?

Types: asset sale · usage fee · subscription fees · lending/renting/leasing · licensing ·
brokerage fees · advertising.
Fixed pricing: list price · product feature dependent · customer segment dependent · volume
dependent. Dynamic pricing: negotiation · yield management · real-time-market.

**Boundary signal:** a capability nobody pays for and nobody stays for is a supporting or generic
candidate no matter how many tables it has. Table count measures effort spent, not value created —
they are frequently inversely related.

## 6. Key Resources

> What Key Resources do our Value Propositions require? Our Distribution Channels? Customer
> Relationships? Revenue Streams?

Types: physical · intellectual (brand, patents, copyrights, data) · human · financial.

**Boundary signal:** intellectual resources — proprietary data, a model, an algorithm — usually sit
inside a core domain. If the answer is "our data on X", find which context owns X.

## 7. Key Activities

> What Key Activities do our Value Propositions require? Our Distribution Channels? Customer
> Relationships? Revenue streams?

Categories: production · problem solving · platform/network.

## 8. Key Partners

> Who are our Key Partners? Who are our key suppliers? Which Key Resources are we acquiring from
> partners? Which Key Activities do partners perform?

Motivations: optimization and economy · reduction of risk and uncertainty · acquisition of
particular resources and activities.

**Boundary signal:** an activity performed by a partner is, almost by definition, generic — the
organisation already decided not to differentiate there. This block hands you generic
classifications nearly for free.

## 9. Cost Structure

> What are the most important costs inherent in our business model? Which Key Resources are most
> expensive? Which Key Activities are most expensive?

Is the business more cost-driven (leanest cost structure, low price value proposition, maximum
automation, extensive outsourcing) or value-driven (focused on value creation, premium value
proposition)? Characteristics: fixed costs · variable costs · economies of scale · economies of
scope.

## Filling honestly

Record a **source per block** — interview (who, when) or document (which file). Two reasons: the
next skill needs to know which claims are evidence and which are inference, and six months from
now nobody can tell them apart from the prose alone.

**Leave blocks empty when nobody knows.** An empty block is a question with a name attached, and
it routes to a person. A plausibly-filled block is a fabrication that three downstream skills will
build on without ever learning it was invented. Sparse and honest beats complete and fictional —
this is the same discipline as never inventing a domain event.

## What the canvas does not tell you

It describes the business, not the users' work. Someone can sell a clear value proposition through
an interface that makes the user's job miserable, and the canvas will look healthy. That is what
the User Story Map is for — see `user-story-mapping.md`.
